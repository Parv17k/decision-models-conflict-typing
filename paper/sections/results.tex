\subsection{Decision models against the LLMs}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main comparison, sorted by accuracy. Asked directly, the \ndm decision models
classify \dmaccmin--\dmaccmax\% of test queries correctly (median \dmaccmedian\%). Gemini~2.5 Flash with the paper's
prompt reaches \gtwofiveacc\% on the same queries. Against it, \ntiedref decision models are statistically tied,
\nbetterref are significantly more accurate (Liquid d1 Ultra, \liquidultravsrefdiff points, $p\liquidultravsrefp$;
Perplexity Decider v1.2, \pplxvsrefdiff, $p\pplxvsrefp$), and \nworseref, ScaleDown Classify-1, is significantly
less accurate (\scaledownvsrefdiff, $p\scaledownvsrefp$). The decision models cost \$\dmcostmin--\$\dmcostmax per
thousand queries (\nfree were free at the time of the runs) against \$\gtwofivecost, and answer in
\dmlatmin--\dmlatmax\,s against \gtwofivelat\,s. Among the \natpar models that match or exceed the paper's LLM,
those that are billed cost \costratiorefmin--\costratiorefmax$\times$ less, and all are
\latratiorefmin--\latratiorefmax$\times$ faster.

The current Gemini~3.8 Flash is stronger: at its lowest thinking level it reaches \gthreeeightacc\%,
\gthreeeightvstwofivediff points above Gemini~2.5 Flash ($p\gthreeeightvstwofivep$), at \costratiorefnow$\times$ its
cost. It is significantly more accurate than \nworsenow of the decision models; the other \ntiednow (Liquid d1
Ultra, Perplexity Decider v1.2 and OpenAI GPT-6 Luna Decisions) trail it by \liquidultravsnowdiffabs to
\lunavsnowdiffabs points without a significant difference. More thinking does not help either Gemini: Gemini~3.8
Flash at its default level scores \gthreeeightdefaultacc\% (\gthinkvsnothinkdiff points against the low level,
$p\gthinkvsnothinkp$) and Gemini~2.5 Flash with thinking \gtwofivethinkacc\% ($p\gtwofivethinkvsnothinkp$).

\paragraph{Reproducing the published number.} On all \nall queries, the scope of the published result, our rerun of
Gemini~2.5 Flash scores \allgtwofive\% against the published 65.3\%. The hosted model may have changed since
publication, and the paper does not specify how results were joined in the prompt or whether thinking was enabled.
We therefore compare every system with our rerun on identical inputs and report the published figure only as
context.

\paragraph{Per class and calibration.} Per class (Table~\ref{tab:perclass}), the models differ most on
\emph{Outdated information}, with F1 from \outdfmin to \outdfmax (Gemini~3.8 Flash: \gthreeeightoutdf).
\emph{Misinformation}, with \nmisitest test instances, defeats every system: no decision model exceeds an F1 of

error of the flat question ranges from \eceminimum to \ecemaximum, and \noverconf of the \ndm models are
over-confident on average (mean top probability above accuracy).

\subsection{Flat or tree?}
\label{sec:formulation}

\input{generated/tab_formulation}

Decomposing the taxonomy into yes/no questions fails for every model. Tree v1 is significantly less accurate than
the flat question for \nvoneworse of the \ndm models (Table~\ref{tab:formulation}). We had inspected the cause on the
development split with one model, \jev~1.13, before any test run: it answers the \emph{compatible} question
\emph{yes} for opinion topics almost as readily as for complementary ones (mean probability \devcompatopin vs.\
\devcompatcomp), sending \devopintocomp of \devnopin development opinion queries to \emph{Complementary}, while the
\emph{opinion} question separates the two classes well (AUROC \devopinionauroc). On the test split the same model
labels \testopintocomp of the \testnopin \emph{Conflicting opinions} queries as \emph{Complementary} (F1
\treeoneopinf). Its \emph{misinformation} question also shows the yes-bias reported for batched yes/no questions
\citep{deusser2026jev}: the mean probability ranged from \devmisinfolo to \devmisinfohi across all five classes.

Tree v2 asks \emph{opinion} first and treats misinformation as the residual factual conflict. Its order was fitted on
that one model's development answers and is applied unchanged to every model. It is significantly better than the
flat question for none of them, tied for \nvtwotied, and significantly worse for \nvtwoworse. For the model it was
fitted on it is tied with flat (\jevtreeacc\% vs.\ \jevflatacc\%, $p\jevtreevsflatp$) and better calibrated (ECE
\jevtreeece vs.\ \jevece). A repair fitted to one model's errors helped no other model, so we use the flat question
for all of them.

\subsection{Confidence-gated cascades}
\label{sec:cascade}

\input{generated/tab_cascade}

Decision models' confidence ranks their own errors, with AUROC from \aurocmin to \aurocmax
(Table~\ref{tab:cascade}), which makes each of them a natural first stage before an LLM. Escalating the least
confident quarter of queries to Gemini~3.8 Flash yields cascades statistically tied with the LLM alone for
\ncasctied of the \ndm models, at \casctiedcostmin--\casctiedcostmax\% of its cost. Escalating by confidence beats
escalating the same number of queries at random for \nbeatrandom of the \ndm models, by up to \cascgainmax points;
the exception is GPT-6 Luna Decisions, whose confidence barely ranks its errors (AUROC \lunaauroc) and whose 25\%
cascade equals random escalation (\casclunatfacc\% vs.\ \casclunatfrand\%). Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} shows every
model alone and as a cascade against the LLMs. A tie at 25\% does not establish equivalence: with \ntest queries, the intervals
still admit losses of a few points relative to the LLM, and showing equivalence at a tight margin would need more
queries than \conflicts provides.

\subsection{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

We specified three hypotheses and their tests before running them; Tables~\ref{tab:diagnostics}
and~\ref{tab:plant} give the results for every decision model's flat question and for Gemini~3.8 Flash. An identical
rerun sets the noise floor: \ndeterministic of the \ndm decision models return identical answers, and the others
change their predicted type on up to \rerunflipmax\% of queries with nothing changed (Gemini~3.8 Flash:
\diaggmrerunflip\%).

\input{generated/tab_diagnostics}

\paragraph{Dates.} One provider documents ordering dates as a known weakness of its model
\citep{typesafe2026jagged}, and we expected date handling to explain errors on \emph{Outdated}. It does not. Removing every date, or adding each result's
recency rank computed in code, changes accuracy significantly in \ndatesig of \ndatetests tests, and recall on
\emph{Outdated} moves little in either direction. The models appear to recognise outdated sources from their
content (phrases such as ``former'' or ``as of'', and conflicting values), not from publication dates.

\paragraph{Source order.} Shuffling the results changes the predicted type on \shuffleflipmin--\shuffleflipmax\% of
queries (pooled over two seeds), above the noise floor for all \ndm models; Gemini~3.8 Flash flips on
\diaggmshuffleflip\%. Paired on the same shuffles, \nflipmore decision models flip significantly more often than the
LLM, \nflipless less often, and \nflipsame no differently. The flips rarely change accuracy: \nshufflesig of
\ndatetests shuffle tests are significant, both for GPT-6 Luna Decisions (\diaglunashuffleonediff and
\diaglunashuffletwodiff points). Order sensitivity is therefore a property of individual models rather than of the
class, and a deployment should test it.

\input{generated/tab_plant}

\paragraph{Planted false sources.} \conflicts has too few \emph{Misinformation} queries to evaluate. We construct
them: in \nplant \emph{No conflict} test queries whose gold answer appears in some result, we rewrite one such
result so that it states a plausible wrong answer, produced by a third model (GPT-4.1-mini) so that no classifier
judges its own text. The expected label becomes \emph{Misinformation}. As hard labels, the decision models
call \planthitmin--\planthitmax\% of planted copies misinformation (Gemini~3.8 Flash: \plantgmhit\%). Net of false
alarms on the clean copies, their detection (Youden index) ranges from \youdenmin to \youdenmax against
\plantgmyouden for the LLM; paired on the same queries, \nyoudenlower are significantly worse and \nyoudentied are
statistically tied. The probabilities tell a different story: the probability of \emph{Misinformation} separates
planted from clean copies with AUROC \plantaurocmin--\plantaurocmax, at least 0.75 for \nplantaurochigh of the
\ndm. The models notice the planted source but rarely make \emph{Misinformation} their top answer, so a lower
threshold on that probability, chosen on development data, would recover detections.

\paragraph{Version stability.} One provider serves two pinned versions. \jev~1.14 differs from 1.13 by
\versionsdiff points with the flat question ($p\versionsp$) and \versionstreediff with tree v2 ($p\versionstreep$),
with the same failure of tree v1 (Table~\ref{tab:formulation}): the conclusions do not depend on which version is
served.

\subsection{End to end: do cheaper types keep the gain?}
\label{sec:endtoend}

With Gemini~2.5 Flash as the generator, \citet{cattan2025dragged} report that the share of answers following the
expected behaviour rises from 65.7\% without a type (Vanilla) to 75.1\% with types predicted by Gemini~2.5 Flash
(Pipeline) and 88.0\% with gold types (Oracle). Can a decision model be the Pipeline's predictor without losing
this gain?

\paragraph{Setup.} The generator is Gemini~2.5 Flash (thinking off, temperature 0) on the same numbered results.
Vanilla uses their answer prompt verbatim; a type adds its definition and expected behaviour from their paper
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). We generate one answer per test query for Vanilla and for each type (\eenanswers
answers); a predictor receives the answer for the type it predicts, so predictors differ only through their
predictions. As in their study, an LLM judge \citep{zheng2023judging} (Gemini~2.5 Flash) decides whether the answer follows the gold type's
expected behaviour, given the query, the type's description, an adherent and a non-adherent example, but not the
sources; a second prompt checks whether it contains the gold answer (\eenrecall queries). On
\nvalid answers labelled blind by two annotators, who agree on \iaa\% (Cohen's $\kappa=\iaakappa$; \citealp{cohen1960kappa}), the judge agrees with
their majority on \eevalacc\% (\citeauthor{cattan2025dragged}: 89\%; $\kappa=\eevalkappa$, as \humanfollows\% of
answers follow; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). Our protocol also specified a second judge, Gemini~3.8 Flash (thinking
low), which agrees less often (\fbvalacc\%); we report both.

\input{generated/tab_endtoend}

\paragraph{Results.} With the primary judge the ordering of \citet{cattan2025dragged} reappears
(Table~\ref{tab:endtoend}): Vanilla \eevan\%, Pipeline \eepipe\% (\eepipevsvandiff points, $p\eepipevsvanp$), Oracle
\eeoracle\% (\eeoraclevsvandiff, $p\eeoraclevsvanp$); our judge rates answers higher than theirs, which compresses
the differences. Replacing Gemini~2.5 Flash as the predictor changes little: \eentied of the \ndm decision models are
tied with it (\eedmdiffmin to \eedmdiffmax points), and only the least accurate classifier, ScaleDown Classify-1, is
significantly worse (\eescaledownvspipediff, $p\eescaledownvspipep$). With a quarter of queries escalated to
Gemini~3.8 Flash, \eencasctied of the \ndm cascades are tied with it (\eecascdiffmin to \eecascdiffmax). Gains
concentrate on \emph{Conflicting opinions} (Vanilla \eevanopin\%, Oracle \eeoracleopin\%) and \emph{Outdated}
(\eevanoutd\% to \eeoracleoutd\%); no condition lowers answer recall (\eerecallmin--\eerecallmax\%). With the second
judge, gold types still help (\fboraclevsvandiff, $p\fboraclevsvanp$) but predicted types do not (Pipeline \fbpipe\%,
Vanilla \fbvan\%, $p\fbpipevsvanp$), and \fbntied of the \ndm decision models remain tied with the Gemini~2.5 Flash
Pipeline. The size of the gain from predicted types thus depends on the judge; that a decision model can replace the
LLM as the predictor does not.
